Active English for Technical Professionals

A speaking bootcamp for technical professionals whose first language is not English and who want faster access to precise, useful language in meetings.

Start with Day 1 →20–30 minutes a day · microphone required · no sign-up

Why I built it

I studied English language teaching and worked as an English teacher before moving into software engineering, test automation, and offensive security.

Over time, I noticed a recurring challenge among many technical professionals whose first language is not English: they understand complex ideas and often recognize advanced vocabulary, but in live conversations they cannot always access the right words quickly enough.

I designed this bootcamp to help move useful vocabulary from passive recognition into spontaneous speech.

This is for you if

  • You read and write English comfortably, but speaking feels slower.
  • You lose precise words when a meeting moves quickly.
  • You want to explain impact, risk, and recommendations clearly.
  • You work in engineering, testing, security, product, data, or another technical field.

How to use the course

Use a real project, incident, meeting, or decision whenever possible. Speak aloud and record yourself. Do not write a perfect script first: the skill you are training is retrieving language while thinking. For each speaking task, make two recordings and keep both.

1 · RetrieveRecall earlier language without notes.
2 · NoticeStudy a few expressions in context.
3 · ApplyConnect them to your real work.
4 · RecordSpeak without reading a script.
5 · ReviewFind pauses, fillers, vague words.
6 · RepeatRecord one improved take.

01Vocabulary Upgrade + BaselineMeasure your current speech and replace vague words with precise alternatives.
25 min90-sec recordingbaseline + phrase bank

Before you start

Open a voice recorder, a timer, and one document or notebook. Use a real situation from your work when possible. Read the steps once, then close the instructions while you speak unless the exercise explicitly asks you to read.

Step by step

  1. Without notes, answer: “What do you work on, and what have you been focusing on recently?” Record 90 seconds. Do not restart.
  2. Listen once. Count fillers, pauses longer than two seconds, repeated words, and moments when you could not retrieve a word.
  3. Study these upgrades: good → effective; bad → ineffective; problem → challenge; important → critical; help → enable.
  4. Create five work-related sentences for each upgraded word and say every sentence aloud.
  5. Record the answer again as context → current focus → result → next step, using at least five target words naturally.

Model answer or starting point

“I work in offensive security. Recently, I have been validating how CI/CD misconfigurations can expose cloud credentials. The work matters because a pipeline weakness can become a path into production infrastructure. My next step is to turn the findings into specific controls for runner permissions and secret handling.”
Useful language: Do not replace words mechanically. Precision matters more than sounding formal.

Self-check

  • You have two 90-second recordings and a written count of fillers, long pauses, repeated words, and retrieval failures.
  • You spoke aloud rather than only writing.
  • You kept the first take, reviewed one or two weaknesses, and made an improved second take.

What to save

Save both recordings and your notes under Day 1 — Vocabulary Upgrade + Baseline. The final item you should have today is: baseline + phrase bank.

03CompressionKeep the central meaning while reducing the amount of language.
20 min2 min → 1 min → 30 secthree recordings

Before you start

Open a voice recorder, a timer, and one document or notebook. Use a real situation from your work when possible. Read the steps once, then close the instructions while you speak unless the exercise explicitly asks you to read.

Step by step

  1. Choose one technical topic, project, or incident you know well.
  2. Explain it in two minutes: context, key detail, impact, and next step.
  3. Explain it in one minute, removing history and implementation detail that does not change the conclusion.
  4. Explain it in 30 seconds. Start with the main point and keep only the consequence and required action.
  5. Check that the shortest version remains accurate and does not exaggerate.

Model answer or starting point

Two minutes: explain the background and method. One minute: keep the finding and evidence. Thirty seconds: “The pipeline can expose temporary cloud credentials to developer-controlled jobs. That creates a path from source control into AWS, so I recommend isolating privileged runners and reducing their role permissions.”
Useful language: Frame: “The short version is… The main issue is… This matters because… What we need to do next is…”

Self-check

  • All three versions state the same accurate conclusion; the shorter versions remove detail rather than speaking faster.
  • You spoke aloud rather than only writing.
  • You kept the first take, reviewed one or two weaknesses, and made an improved second take.

  2. Answer “What is the issue?” Name the situation and consequence in 20 seconds.
  3. Answer “What is your recommendation?” State the action first, then one reason.
